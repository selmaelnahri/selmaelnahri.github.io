Observation became embedded within everyday life. Cameras and monitoring systems transformed visibility into a method of control, shaping behaviour through the possibility of being watched.

Surveillance has long been linked to systems of social control. While monitoring technologies are often introduced in the name of safety or efficiency, they also reshape the relationship between individuals and power.

As surveillance becomes embedded within everyday environments, observation shifts from an occasional event to a continuous condition of modern life. Cameras, digital networks, and data systems record behaviour across public and private spaces, producing vast archives of information about individuals and their movements.

Visibility is no longer only imposed. Cameras now function as tools for performance, identity, and self-presentation, encouraging participation within systems of observation.

Social media has transformed surveillance into something participatory. Platforms encourage users to constantly document themselves through images, livestreams, and personal updates, turning visibility into a form of social interaction and performance.

Cameras are no longer only associated with monitoring or control, but with identity, entertainment, and self-presentation.

The same technologies used for facial recognition and behavioural tracking are also used to unlock phones, apply beauty filters, and broadcast everyday life to online audiences. Surveillance has become embedded within ordinary routines, making observation feel less like an external threat and more like a normal condition of contemporary life.
